% Abstract

\begin{abstract}
Telugu handwriting recognition remains difficult, largely because of the script's syllabic structure. Consonants fuse with vowel diacritics and stack into conjuncts via the virama; the resulting compound glyphs bear little visual resemblance to their constituent characters. We compare a CNN-BiLSTM-CTC baseline with an autoregressive Transformer decoder trained under a joint CTC-attention loss on the IIIT-HW-Telugu benchmark (80k training images, 17,910 test). Both decoders share a stride-modified ResNet-18 encoder that preserves horizontal resolution for sequential decoding. We also construct a character bigram transition matrix from the training labels and apply it as a soft constraint during inference. The best Transformer configuration achieves 3.66\% CER, a 6.4\% relative reduction over the CTC baseline's 3.91\%. Two details proved critical for convergence: scaling token embeddings by $\sqrt{d_{\text{model}}}$ and tying input--output embedding weights. An earlier decoder lacking both still trained but plateaued at 4.89\% CER---below the CTC baseline---though that comparison confounds several hyperparameters and is not an isolated ablation. Error analysis reveals a practical trade-off. CTC scatters small substitutions across many samples; the Transformer is more accurate on average but occasionally exhibits what we term \textit{autoregressive collapse}, confidently emitting a valid Telugu word unrelated to the input image. Code and trained weights are available; see the Reproducibility section.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Handwritten text recognition (HTR) for Latin scripts now routinely achieves single-digit character error rates on standard benchmarks such as IAM and RIMES~\cite{vaswani2017attention, li2023trocr}. Indic scripts lag behind. Telugu, spoken natively by over 80~million people and designated one of India's classical languages~\cite{ethnologue2024}, has received comparatively little HTR attention relative to its speaker population.

The difficulty is largely structural. Telugu has 36~base consonants (\textit{hallulu}), 16~vowels (\textit{acchulu}), and vowel diacritics (\textit{matras}) that attach to consonant glyphs and alter their shape. The virama character (\textit{halant}, U+0C4D) stacks consonants into conjuncts (\textit{vattu} forms), producing compound glyphs that can be visually unrelated to their constituent consonants. Latin letters, by contrast, retain roughly the same shape regardless of context.

Current HTR systems generally follow one of two decoding strategies. CTC~\cite{graves2006ctc} avoids character-level bounding boxes by having the encoder emit a distribution at every time step; dynamic programming then marginalises over all alignments consistent with the target label sequence. CTC is fast and effective for left-to-right scripts, but each output position is predicted independently. Autoregressive (AR) Transformer decoders~\cite{vaswani2017attention} generate characters one at a time, conditioning each prediction on the full history of previously emitted tokens. This enables the decoder to capture inter-character dependencies---for example, that a particular vowel diacritic rarely follows a certain consonant cluster in Telugu---at the cost of slower inference and exposure bias, where an early decoding error propagates into all subsequent predictions.

With sufficient pre-training data, Transformers outperform CTC on English HTR. TrOCR~\cite{li2023trocr} and PARSeq~\cite{bautista2022parseq} demonstrated this on benchmarks comprising millions of images. For low-resource scripts the evidence is less clear: Gongidi and Jawahar~\cite{gongidi2021iiit} observed that CTC remains competitive on Indic languages when labelled data is scarce. This motivates the central question of our work: \textit{given roughly 80k Telugu word images, does a Transformer decoder outperform a well-tuned CTC baseline, and by how much?}

Specifically, we make the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A shared ResNet-18 visual encoder with modified stride patterns that keeps horizontal resolution intact for both the CTC and Transformer decoders.
    
    \item An autoregressive Transformer decoder trained with a joint CTC-attention loss, along with two architectural details -- embedding scaling and weight tying -- that in our experience separated a decoder that merely trains from one that outperforms the CTC baseline.
    
    \item A data-driven Telugu character transition matrix built from training-set bigram counts, tested here as a soft decoding constraint.
    
    \item To our knowledge, the lowest lexicon-free CER reported on the IIIT-HW-Telugu benchmark~\cite{gongidi2021iiit}: 3.66\%, down from the 4.58\% that Dutta~et~al.~\cite{dutta2018} obtained.

    \item A methodological caution for Indic HTR: because conditioning on the presence of a virama also conditions on word length, CER computed over compound and simple subsets is not directly comparable, and the apparent advantage on compound words disappears once measured at the word level.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{CTC-Based Handwriting Recognition}

CTC~\cite{graves2006ctc} remains the dominant loss for HTR because it requires only image--transcript pairs, not character-level bounding boxes. The CRNN pipeline of Shi~et~al.~\cite{shi2017crnn}---convolutional features fed into a BiLSTM and decoded with CTC---established the standard baseline architecture. Puigcerver~\cite{puigcerver2017} later demonstrated that width-preserving convolutions improve the encoder, and Scheidl~et~al.~\cite{scheidl2018word} integrated language-model-aware beam search into CTC decoding. Individually these refinements yield modest gains, but collectively they have kept CTC competitive with attention-based alternatives.

\subsection{Attention and Transformer-Based HTR}

Transformer architectures entered HTR via scene-text recognition. TrOCR~\cite{li2023trocr} combines a pre-trained ViT encoder with a GPT-2-style decoder and reports strong results on several English benchmarks; its performance, however, depends on large-scale pre-training corpora that do not exist for most Indic scripts. PARSeq~\cite{bautista2022parseq} trains a single model on every permutation of character order, enabling both left-to-right and parallel decoding. To our knowledge, neither system has been evaluated on Telugu, so whether their gains transfer to a syllabic script remains open.

\subsection{Indic Script Recognition}

Published work on Telugu HTR specifically is limited. Dutta~et~al.~\cite{dutta2018} trained CNN-RNN-CTC pipelines on IIIT-HW-Telugu and reported 9.15\% CER, reduced to 4.58\% with IAM pre-training. Gongidi and Jawahar~\cite{gongidi2021iiit} introduced the broader IIIT-INDIC-HW-WORDS benchmark and obtained 6.41\% CER with a CRNN; lexicon-constrained decoding lowered this to 1.52\%, though such decoding is limited to a closed vocabulary. The PLATTER system~\cite{platter2025} addressed page-level Indic HTR but evaluated only on Hindi and Bangla, not Telugu.

\subsection{Joint CTC-Attention Training}

Watanabe~et~al.~\cite{watanabe2017hybrid} demonstrated in speech recognition that an auxiliary CTC loss alongside an attention decoder accelerates convergence. The CTC branch encourages the encoder to produce roughly time-aligned features, giving the attention mechanism a simpler alignment to learn. Michael~et~al.~\cite{michael2019evaluating} applied the same joint-loss formulation to HTR and reported faster convergence and fewer attention-collapse episodes relative to pure attention training. We adopt this formulation for our Transformer decoder.

\section{Proposed Method}

The system comprises four components, illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}: a shared CNN feature encoder, a CTC baseline decoder, an autoregressive Transformer decoder with an auxiliary CTC head, and an optional Telugu character-transition mask applied during inference.

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/system_architecture.jpg}
    \caption{System overview. The stride-patched ResNet-18 encoder turns a word image into a 64-step feature sequence, keeping the layer~3--4 strides at $(2,1)$ rather than $(2,2)$. Two decoder paths then share this representation: \textbf{Path~A} runs it through a 2-layer BiLSTM for CTC decoding, while \textbf{Path~B} refines it with a 3-layer Transformer encoder, branches off an auxiliary CTC head ($\lambda{=}0.3$), and decodes autoregressively with a 6-layer Transformer decoder. An optional Telugu grammar mask penalises unlikely character transitions at inference time.}
    \label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{CNN Feature Encoder}

The visual encoder is a ResNet-18~\cite{he2016resnet} pre-trained on ImageNet, with modified spatial downsampling. An unmodified ResNet-18 reduces both height and width by a factor of 32; for a 512-pixel-wide input this would leave only 16~horizontal positions, insufficient for CTC or attention decoding of a typical 8--10 character Telugu word. We address this by setting the stride of the first convolution in layers~3 and~4 from $(2,2)$ to $(2,1)$:
\begin{equation}
    \text{layer3}[0].\text{stride}: (2,2) \rightarrow (2,1)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \text{layer4}[0].\text{stride}: (2,2) \rightarrow (2,1)
\end{equation}

Height is still reduced, but horizontal resolution is preserved, yielding feature maps of shape $[B, 512, 4, W/8]$. The input is grayscale, replicated to three channels to match the pre-trained weights. A $1 \times 1$ convolution projects the 512~channels to $d_{\text{model}}$, adaptive average pooling collapses the height dimension, and the result is a sequence of $T = W/8 = 64$ feature vectors. Sinusoidal positional encodings~\cite{vaswani2017attention} are added to preserve ordering information.

\subsection{CTC Baseline Architecture}

For the CTC baseline, we stack a 2-layer bidirectional LSTM (hidden size~256, giving 512-dimensional concatenated states) on top of the shared encoder features, then project those states with a linear head to logits over the 91-token vocabulary. Training uses CTC loss~\cite{graves2006ctc}, with the PAD token (index~0) doubling as the CTC blank. BiLSTM weights are initialized with the orthogonal scheme, as is standard practice. At test time, decoding is greedy, collapsing repeated tokens and blanks in a single pass.

\subsection{Autoregressive Transformer Decoder}

The Transformer decoder follows Vaswani~et~al.~\cite{vaswani2017attention} with several modifications. Each of the $N$ decoder layers contains masked self-attention, cross-attention over the encoder memory, and a feed-forward block with GELU activations. Layer normalisation is applied \textit{before} each sub-layer (Pre-LN), as recommended by Xiong~et~al.~\cite{xiong2020layer} for gradient stability in deep Transformers. An optional lightweight 3-layer Transformer encoder refines the CNN features before they reach the decoder's cross-attention heads.

Two architectural details proved critical for convergence on our Telugu dataset:

\textbf{Embedding Scaling.} Scaling token embeddings by $\sqrt{d_{\text{model}}}$, as originally proposed by Vaswani~et~al.~\cite{vaswani2017attention}, proved necessary for our Telugu vocabulary:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{e}_t = \text{Embed}(y_t) \cdot \sqrt{d_{\text{model}}} + \text{PE}(t)
\end{equation}
Without this factor, embedding entries are initialised at considerably smaller magnitude than the sinusoidal positional encoding, and inspection of the resulting attention maps suggests the decoder attends based on position rather than token identity. We state this as an interpretation, not a measured causal mechanism. In either case, the failure mode is expected to be particularly damaging for Telugu, where word length varies widely and the same character recurs across many positions.

\textbf{Weight Tying.} Following Press and Wolf~\cite{press2017output}, we tie the weight matrix between the input embedding layer and the output softmax projection:
\begin{equation}
    P(y_t | y_{<t}, \mathbf{h}) = \text{softmax}(\mathbf{W}_{\text{embed}} \cdot \mathbf{d}_t)
\end{equation}
This halves the embedding parameter count and forces each token vector to serve both as an input representation and an output predictor, acting as an implicit regulariser. The effect was consistent on our 91-token vocabulary.

\textbf{Joint CTC-CE Training.} Training uses a weighted sum of cross-entropy and an auxiliary CTC loss computed on the encoder output:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = (1 - \lambda) \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} + \lambda \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{CTC}}
    \label{eq:joint_loss}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda = 0.3$. The cross-entropy term includes label smoothing ($\epsilon = 0.05$, following Szegedy~et~al.~\cite{szegedy2016rethinking}). The CTC branch encourages the encoder to produce features that are already roughly aligned with target characters, which in turn allows the attention decoder to converge faster and more reliably.

\subsection{Telugu Linguistic Constraint Mask}

Telugu, like most languages, has phonotactic constraints -- not every character can follow every other character. We exploit this property by scanning the training transcripts, recording the character bigrams that actually occur, and storing the results in a binary matrix $\mathbf{M} \in \{0, 1\}^{|V| \times |V|}$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{M}[i][j] = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if bigram } (c_i, c_j) \text{ observed in training} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
\end{equation}

At each decoding step $t$, we look up the row corresponding to the most recently generated token $c_{t-1}$ and subtract a large penalty $\delta = 10.0$ from the logits of any character that was never observed following $c_{t-1}$ in the training data:
\begin{equation}
    \ell'_j = \ell_j - \delta \cdot (1 - \mathbf{M}[c_{t-1}][j])
\end{equation}
We use a soft penalty rather than a hard mask deliberately, allowing the decoder to emit rare but potentially valid pairs that may simply not have appeared in the 80,000 training words.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}

We measure accuracy with two standard metrics. Character Error Rate~(CER) is the total edit distance summed across all test samples, divided by the total number of ground-truth characters:
\begin{equation}
    \text{CER} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} \text{ED}(\hat{y}_i, y_i)}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} |y_i|}
\end{equation}
where $\text{ED}(\cdot, \cdot)$ denotes Levenshtein distance. Word Error Rate~(WER) is just the fraction of words where the prediction contains any error, however small. To quantify the uncertainty on each system's CER we use non-parametric bootstrap resampling~\cite{efron1993bootstrap} over test samples with 1,000 resamples, and report 95\% percentile confidence intervals. These are marginal intervals for each system evaluated independently; they are not a paired test of the difference between two systems, and we are careful below not to read significance directly off whether they overlap.

We highlight three findings from these results (see also Figs.~\ref{fig:training} and~\ref{fig:ablation}):

\noindent\textbf{(1) The Transformer outperforms CTC, but convergence required specific architectural choices.} The best configuration (Row~4, beam-5, unconstrained) achieves 3.66\% CER against the CTC baseline's 3.91\%, a 6.4\% relative reduction. The two bootstrap intervals abut rather than overlap ([3.54,~3.79] vs.~[3.79,~4.04]), and the gap is consistent across all four Transformer variants and on both metrics, making a sampling artefact unlikely; a paired bootstrap over per-sample differences would be the stricter test, and we regard the current evidence as suggestive rather than conclusive. We note that our initial Transformer runs produced CER above the CTC baseline; performance improved only after adding embedding scaling and weight tying (Section~III-C).

\noindent\textbf{(2) Beam search yields negligible gains.} Greedy decoding achieves 3.67\% CER; beam width 5 reduces this by only 0.01 percentage point. The confidence intervals overlap almost entirely, suggesting that encoder features, not the search strategy, are the binding constraint. Given the 55$\times$ increase in per-image latency, greedy decoding is the preferable operating point.

\noindent\textbf{(3) The grammar mask produces mixed effects.} The Telugu transition matrix increases greedy CER from 3.67\% to 3.74\%, likely because it penalises rare-but-valid character pairs absent from the 80k training words. Constrained beam search, however, achieves the lowest WER across all configurations (23.28\%), indicating that the mask suppresses some whole-word errors at the cost of additional character-level substitutions.

By CER alone, both models appear to perform \textit{better} on compound words than on simple ones---a counter-intuitive result. One might conclude that conjunct glyphs, despite their visual complexity, are sufficiently distinctive to be recognised easily. The WER column contradicts this: at the word level, both models perform slightly \textit{worse} on compound words (CTC 25.72\% vs.\ 23.60\%, AR 23.47\% vs.\ 23.18\%).

The discrepancy is a length effect. Compound words in this test set average 9.87 characters against 7.23 for simple words, roughly 36\% longer. Because CER divides accumulated edit distance by total ground-truth characters, a longer word spreads the same word-level mistake over a larger denominator and so registers a lower CER. Normalising for this, the mean edit distance per erroneous word is nearly identical across the two subsets (1.39 characters for compound, 1.33 for simple). We flag this because subset CER comparisons are common in Indic HTR papers, and where the subsets differ systematically in word length -- which is exactly what conditioning on the presence of a virama does -- CER differences between them are not directly interpretable as differences in difficulty.

What the breakdown does show cleanly is \textit{where} the Transformer earns its advantage. On compound words it cuts CER from 3.70\% to 3.31\%, a 10.5\% relative reduction, and WER from 25.72\% to 23.47\%. On simple words the two models are indistinguishable (4.29\% vs.\ 4.28\% CER). Practically all of the Transformer's aggregate gain therefore comes from conjunct-bearing words, which is consistent with the idea that modelling dependencies between adjacent characters helps most where those dependencies are structurally strongest.

\subsection{Qualitative Error Analysis}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/confusion_matrix_ar_v2.png}
    \caption{Character-level confusion matrix for the AR Transformer on the test set. Darker cells mean more frequent confusions. Most of the errors fall between consonant pairs that share stroke structure (e.g., \textit{dda}$\leftrightarrow$\textit{da}) and vowel signs that differ by only a single stroke.}
    \label{fig:confusion}
\end{figure}

The aggregate CER numbers mask an important distinction: the two architectures produce qualitatively different error patterns. Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion} shows the Transformer's character confusion matrix; Fig.~\ref{fig:examples} presents representative predictions from both models.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/prediction_examples.png}
    \caption{Side-by-side predictions from CTC and the AR Transformer. The top rows are cases both models get right; the bottom rows show typical failures. CTC tends to swap visually similar consonants, while the Transformer occasionally hallucinates a fluent-sounding but completely wrong word.}
    \label{fig:examples}
\end{figure}

\textbf{CTC errors} are predominantly \textit{local substitutions}---one or two characters replaced by visually similar alternatives. The most frequent confusions involve consonant pairs with similar stroke geometry (\textit{dda}~$\leftrightarrow$~\textit{da}, 478 cases in our test set) and vowel-sign pairs differing by a single stroke (\textit{e-matra}~$\leftrightarrow$~\textit{i-matra}, 169 cases). Because each CTC prediction depends only on the encoder and not on prior outputs, these errors are distributed relatively uniformly across the test set and rarely corrupt more than two characters per word.

\textbf{Transformer errors} follow a different pattern. The decoder recognises most words correctly, accounting for its lower overall CER, but its failures tend to be severe. We refer to this failure mode as \textit{autoregressive collapse}: the decoder confidently outputs a valid Telugu word that bears no relation to the input image. In one observed case, four visually distinct input images all produced the same common-word prediction. The mechanism is straightforward: if the first character is decoded incorrectly, the decoder's self-attention over its own output history reinforces the error, producing a coherent but entirely wrong character sequence. CTC is structurally immune to this failure mode because its predictions carry no feedback from prior outputs.

\subsection{Validation-Test Gap}

The Transformer's best validation CER was 2.40\% (epoch~72), substantially lower than the 3.67\% observed on the test set. We attribute this gap to three factors:

\textbf{(1) Writer mismatch.} The test set likely contains handwriting styles underrepresented in the validation split. The Transformer, with more parameters than the CTC model, is more susceptible to overfitting to writer-specific characteristics.

\textbf{(2) Checkpoint selection bias.} Selecting the checkpoint with the lowest validation CER from 80~epochs introduces a mild form of overfitting to the validation distribution.

\textbf{(3) Limited training data.} Systems such as TrOCR~\cite{li2023trocr} and PARSeq~\cite{bautista2022parseq} train on millions of images with additional pre-training. Our 80k samples may be insufficient for the Transformer to generalise as broadly as it could with a larger corpus.

% VI. DISCUSSION
% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{Discussion}

The CTC-versus-Transformer comparison is less straightforward than the headline CER numbers indicate.

CTC has a structural advantage: it assumes left-to-right character order and that encoder outputs are already roughly aligned with them. This built-in prior acts as free regularisation and reduces training cost---CTC reaches 3.91\% CER in 50~epochs (3.5~hours), whereas the Transformer requires 80~epochs (5.4~hours) to reach 3.66\%. The Transformer must learn left-to-right alignment through cross-attention. We emphasise that this is an observation about training budget, not a controlled data-efficiency experiment: we did not sweep training-set size and cannot determine at what scale the two curves would cross.

The autoregressive collapse described in Section~\ref{sec:results} has practical implications. In a pipeline digitising historical Telugu manuscripts or legal records, a model that occasionally produces a fluent but entirely incorrect word is more problematic than one that makes frequent small substitutions, because the errors are harder to detect during review. For such applications CTC may be the safer deployment choice, whereas the Transformer is preferable when minimising average CER is the primary objective.

The Telugu grammar mask did not improve CER. This is expected in retrospect: the transition matrix was built exclusively from training-set bigrams, so any valid character pair absent from those 80k words receives a penalty at test time. A mask derived from formal phonological rules or from a larger unlabelled text corpus would likely perform better.

\subsection{Limitations}

Three limitations should be noted. First, our observation that embedding scaling and weight tying are necessary for convergence is based on our training runs rather than a controlled ablation with all other variables fixed; we report it as a practical engineering finding rather than a measured causal effect. A related ablation on an earlier architecture version removed the auxiliary CTC loss and saw CER rise from 4.89\% to 5.43\%, supporting the joint-loss design, though those numbers come from a different configuration and are not directly comparable to Table~\ref{tab:ablation}. Second, the 1.27-point gap between best validation CER (2.40\%) and test CER (3.67\%) is non-trivial, and because the IIIT-HW-Telugu splits are not writer-disjoint by construction, we cannot fully disentangle writer overfitting from checkpoint-selection bias. Third, our comparison against published systems is not controlled, for the reasons given in Section~V-B.

\subsection{Future Work}

Several directions follow from this work. Pre-training the Transformer decoder on synthetic Telugu text rendered from digital fonts could supply language-modelling knowledge without additional handwritten data. Non-autoregressive decoders such as PARSeq~\cite{bautista2022parseq} may combine the Transformer's contextual modelling with CTC-style parallel decoding, avoiding exposure bias. An ensemble of CTC and Transformer outputs is also worth investigating, given that their error profiles are largely complementary.

% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
% VII. CONCLUSION
% ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{Conclusion}

This paper compared CTC and autoregressive Transformer decoders for Telugu word-level handwriting recognition, using a shared stride-modified ResNet-18 encoder for a controlled comparison. The Transformer, trained with a joint CTC-attention loss, achieves 3.66\% CER on the IIIT-HW-Telugu test set---to our knowledge the lowest lexicon-free result reported on this benchmark, and a 20\% relative reduction from the 4.58\% of Dutta~et~al.~\cite{dutta2018}. Two architectural details---embedding scaling by $\sqrt{d_{\text{model}}}$ and weight tying---were essential for convergence in our experiments. Decomposing results by word type revealed that the Transformer's advantage comes entirely from conjunct-bearing words, and also that subset CER comparisons are confounded by systematic word-length differences, a point worth considering when interpreting compound-versus-simple results in Indic HTR. Finally, we observe a deployment trade-off: CTC produces frequent small character-level errors, while the Transformer is more accurate on average but susceptible to autoregressive collapse. Code and trained models are publicly available.

\section*{Reproducibility}

All source code, configuration files, and training scripts are released at \url{https://github.com/Bhargav-252006/telugu_htr}.
